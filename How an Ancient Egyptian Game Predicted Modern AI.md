It was a long time ago when the ancient Egyptians played a game called Senet. The game featured 30 blocks distributed in a 3x10 grid—3 rows and 10 columns.

The game pieces, which historians call ludemes, moved along a winding, S-shaped curve.

To the ancient Egyptians, this wasn't just a fun board game to pass the time. It was a two-player battle where each player had 5 pieces to command (usually five light pieces and five dark pieces). They genuinely believed it was a simulation of the soul’s journey through the dangerous underworld to reach eternal life.

But who could have known that thousands of years later, this simple pastime would become the physical blueprint for one of the most popular tools in artificial intelligence today: the Decision Tree?

### A Game Built on Branches

![](https://cdn.hashnode.com/uploads/covers/62553c746386ed96b050c35c/ed7b1ddc-1235-4b80-823d-79e33a69c0bb.jpg align="center")

Senet works on a very simple concept of "If-Then" choices. Instead of modern dice, players threw four flat casting sticks. Depending on how the sticks landed, you could move your pieces forward.

Because of the rules, you couldn't just move blindly. You had to look at the board, look at your 5 pieces, and ask yourself questions:

• If I choose Option 1 and move my first piece, then it will land on a safe square. • If I choose Option 2 and move my second piece, then it might fall into the "House of Water"—a trap that drowns your piece and sends it all the way back to the start.

Every single turn created a splitting path of choices and consequences. The Egyptians didn't have computers, but their brains were manually running a decision-making system. They were mapping out a tree of choices in their heads.

### Enter the Mathematicians (1654)

For centuries, understanding the math behind these branching paths was impossible for the general public. People just blamed their wins or losses on "luck" or the gods.

That changed in 1654 because of a gambling argument in France.

A nobleman wanted to know how to fairly split the prize money of a dice game if it had to be stopped halfway through.

To solve this, two brilliant mathematicians stepped up: Blaise Pascal and Pierre de Fermat. They had never heard of Senet (which was still buried in Egyptian tombs at the time), but they tackled the exact same logic.

They sat down and literally drew out branching paths on paper to calculate the future.

• If Player A wins the next round, this happens.

• If Player B wins, that happens.

By assigning numerical odds to each branch, Pascal and Fermat invented Probability Theory and created the world's first mathematical "probability trees." They gave us the language to calculate risk.

### From Board Games to Boardrooms

The evolutionary line didn't stop there. This exact concept of branching paths eventually became the foundation for teaching computers how to play Chess.

Just like a Senet player mapping out a move, early AI scientists used massive decision trees to help computers calculate millions of potential chess variations.

Finally, in the mid-20th century, scientists figured out how to make computers build these trees automatically from data.

The modern AI Decision Tree was born.Today, decision trees are the unsung heroes of our digital world.

• When you apply for a credit card, a decision tree checks your income to approve you

• When a bank looks for scammers, a tree blocks suspicious accounts.

• When a doctor inputs your symptoms, a tree helps diagnose your illness.

### What If They Disappeared?

If decision trees suddenly vanished from our software, the modern world would hit a massive speed bump.

Without them, streaming apps like Netflix would struggle to recommend movies instantly. High-tech medicine would slow down, forcing doctors to guess diagnoses manually. Banks would take weeks to approve simple loans, and artificial intelligence would lose its ability to explain why it made a choice. We would lose our simplest tool for making smart, automated decisions.

### The Timeless Tree of Choices

Whether you are an ancient Egyptian pharaoh trying to navigate a wooden board with your 5 pieces, a French mathematician solving a dice dispute, or a modern AI predicting the stock market, the underlying engine is exactly the same.

Human beings have always looked for ways to map the future. Senet was simply humanity’s first physical prototype of a decision tree—proving that the basic logic we use to power artificial intelligence today is as old as civilization itself.
